2. DRY-RUN THE REAL DEPENDENCY GRAPH
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Do not simply reread slices numerically.

Construct the effective implementation dependency DAG and simulate the work in
an order that could actually be executed.

For every slice determine:

- what authoritative state exists before it starts;
- which earlier contracts it assumes;
- which schemas/types/interfaces it consumes;
- which persisted formats it reads or mutates;
- which events/jobs/lifecycle states it depends upon;
- which adapters/surfaces consume its result;
- what migration state may coexist during rollout;
- what happens after process restart;
- what happens with two clients;
- what happens with concurrent writers/jobs;
- what happens if the slice succeeds only partially;
- what constitutes durable completion;
- what evidence permits the next dependent slice to begin;
- what rollback actually means after later work has occurred.

Look for hidden dependencies.

Examples:

A UI slice may appear to depend only on configuration APIs but actually depend
on lifecycle states introduced elsewhere.

A persistence migration may require the new identity contract before it can
safely produce converted records.

A retrieval contract may depend upon indexes whose revision semantics have not
yet been created.

A compatibility adapter may be needed before the canonical contract can be
switched.

A scheduler change may depend upon job identity/recovery semantics defined in a
different slice.

Find these now.

Update dependency declarations where necessary.
